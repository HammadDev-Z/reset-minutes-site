In a small home, the pet bowls, the litter and the cleaning cupboard are never far apart. This guide collects what the EPA, the FDA and the ASPCA say about using household cleaning products around pets. It puts the source beside each point and says plainly what those sources do **not** cover. It is general information, not veterinary advice.

::: safety
**If your pet may have swallowed or been exposed to a cleaning product:** call your veterinarian or a pet poison control center right away. The FDA lists two: the Pet Poison Helpline, 855-764-7661, and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center, 888-426-4435 ([FDA: potentially dangerous items for your pet](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/potentially-dangerous-items-your-pet), content current as of 28 Feb 2023). The EPA says to have the product label with you when you call ([EPA: Read the label first](https://www.epa.gov/pets/read-label-first-protect-your-pets)). The pages we read do not say whether these lines charge a fee, so ask when you call.
:::

### Read the label and follow its pet precautions {#read-the-label}

The EPA says to read the label before you buy, store and use household cleaners, and that following its warnings and directions helps keep you and your pets safe. Labels also carry first aid instructions and phone numbers for help ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pets/read-label-first-protect-your-pets), updated 23 Mar 2026). The EPA's pet brochure adds: keep pets away from treated areas as directed on the label ([EPA brochure](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-02/documents/pets.pdf)).

### Spills and treated areas {#spills-and-treated-areas}

The EPA says that if there is a spill, you should keep animals out of the area until it is cleaned up. The ASPCA says most cleaning products are safe around dogs and cats when used according to the label ([ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/poisonous-household-products)).

### Food and water dishes, and storage {#bowls-and-storage}

The EPA says not to spray or store cleaning products near pet food or water dishes, and to store all household cleaning products out of reach of pets. It also says to keep products in their original containers, because loose lids can spill and let a pet reach the product, and throwing away the original container throws away emergency information ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pets/read-label-first-protect-your-pets)). For latches, locks and other storage rules from the CPSC and Poison Control, see our guide to [storing cleaning products in a small home](/guides/store-cleaning-products-safely-small-home/).

### Let surfaces dry before pets return {#let-surfaces-dry}

The ASPCA gives product-specific points. Enzyme-based cleaners usually cause only mild stomach upset, and it says to let them dry before the pet enters the area. A vinegar and water mix is acidic and can upset the stomach and irritate tissue, but it says this is generally not a problem if the mix is diluted, rinsed and allowed to dry before use. For cleaning pet toys and kennels with diluted bleach, it says to rinse thoroughly and make sure the odor has dissipated before the pet returns ([ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/poisonous-household-products)). It gives no drying times.

### Products that need extra care {#products-needing-extra-care}

- **Undiluted bleach** can injure the mouth and esophagus, especially at high concentration or high pH ([ASPCA](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/poisonous-household-products)). The FDA lists various household cleaners, including bleach and toilet bowl cleaners, among non-edible items that may be dangerous to pets ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/potentially-dangerous-items-your-pet)).
- **Dryer sheets and fabric softeners** mostly contain cationic detergents, which the ASPCA says can cause severe chemical burns and ulcers in the mouth, esophagus and stomach. It says dryer sheets can also cause a blockage, which is a medical emergency.
- **Laundry detergent pods and liquid laundry detergent** can cause stomach upset and mouth and throat irritation, and in some cases serious, life-threatening symptoms, according to the ASPCA.
- **Birds** are extremely sensitive to fumes, and the ASPCA says to get veterinary advice before using cleaning products around them.

## If your pet may have been exposed {#if-your-pet-is-exposed}

Call your vet or a pet poison control center right away; the FDA page lists the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661) and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435). The EPA says to keep your veterinarian's or local animal hospital's number and your local poison control number easy to reach, and to have the product label with you when you call. The ASPCA page says its guide is general information and is not meant to assess or manage time-sensitive exposures. The pages we read give no step-by-step first aid for pets, so we do not either.

## Setting this up in a small home (our suggestions) {#small-home-setup}

These are **our suggestions** for applying the points above. They are not statements from the sources.

- **Clean the pet area while the pet is in another room,** and keep them out until the surface is dry or the label's directions say it is safe. This follows the EPA's "keep pets away from treated areas as directed on the label."
- **Move pet bowls out of the spray zone before you start,** and wash your hands before refilling them.
- **Keep the product label or a photo of it,** so you have it ready if you ever need to call.
- **Put the number list where you will see it,** for example inside a cabinet door or on the fridge.
- **On a rough day, use the [minimum day](/guides/minimum-day-cleaning-routine/),** which needs no cleaning product beyond the dish soap you already use.

## What the sources do not say {#what-the-sources-do-not-say}

- **No list of "pet-safe" products.** None of the pages we read names products as safe for pets, and we do not either.
- **No drying times or dilution ratios.** The ASPCA says to rinse and let some products dry or air out, but it gives no times or ratios.
- **No step-by-step first aid.** The pages we read say to call a vet or poison center; they do not describe what to do before you get through.
- **No advice for every species.** Apart from birds, the pages we read do not give species-specific cleaning advice. Ask your vet about fish, reptiles, small mammals and other pets.
- **No fee information** for the poison lines, in the pages we read.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are cleaning products safe around dogs and cats?**
The ASPCA says most are safe around dogs and cats when used according to the label. The EPA says to follow label warnings and keep pets away from treated areas as the label directs.

**How long should I keep my pet away after cleaning?**
The sources give no times. The EPA says to follow the label's directions on treated areas, and the ASPCA says to let some products dry or air out first.

**Is it okay to spray near the pet's bowls?**
No. The EPA says not to spray or store cleaning products near pet food or water dishes.

**What if I have a bird?**
The ASPCA says birds are extremely sensitive to fumes and to get veterinary advice before using cleaning products around them.

**Who do I call if my pet swallows a cleaner?**
Your vet, or a pet poison control center. The FDA page lists the Pet Poison Helpline (855-764-7661) and the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center (888-426-4435).

**Is this veterinary advice?**
No. It is general information taken from the sources listed below.
