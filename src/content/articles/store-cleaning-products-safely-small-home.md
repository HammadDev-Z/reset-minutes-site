Small homes have small cabinets, and the cleaner often ends up under the kitchen sink, next to the cooking oil. This guide pulls together what three official sources say about storing household cleaning products, shows the source beside each rule, and says plainly what those sources do **not** cover. It is general information, not medical advice.

::: safety
**If a child swallows or drinks a cleaner or detergent:** call the poison center at 1-800-222-1222 in the US, or use the web POISON CONTROL tool. Poison Control says this help is free, confidential and available 24 hours a day ([Poison Control: safe spring cleaning](https://www.poison.org/articles/spring-cleaning)).
:::

### Latched or locked, and out of sight {#locked-or-latched}

Poison Control says to keep cleaners in cabinets with child-resistant latches, and calls an unlocked, low kitchen or bathroom cabinet one of the most dangerous places to store them ([Poison Control](https://www.poison.org/articles/spring-cleaning)). The CPSC says to store household products in cabinets with child safety latches or locks, and to keep anything poisonous locked up and out of sight ([CPSC publication 382](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/382.pdf)). Its poison prevention page says to lock chemicals and cleaning supplies in a cabinet or box, out of children's reach ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Poison-Prevention-Information-Center)). The same publication says to reseal child-resistant packaging after every use, and says nearly 1 million children under five are exposed each year to potentially poisonous medicines and household chemicals.

### Original containers only {#original-containers}

Both the CPSC and the EPA say to keep products in the containers they came in. The CPSC says never to put bleach and similar products in cups, glasses, or milk or soft drink bottles, and never to move a dangerous product into a bottle without a child-resistant closure ([CPSC publication 382](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/382.pdf)). The EPA calls putting products in food or drink containers extremely dangerous, says loose lids can spill, and says that throwing away the original container also throws away its emergency information ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pets/read-label-first-protect-your-pets), updated 23 Mar 2026). If a label tells you to mix a product in another container, the EPA says to use the whole mixture or, if you cannot, to label the new container clearly.

### Away from food, medicines and pet bowls {#away-from-food-and-pets}

The CPSC says cleaning fluids, detergents and similar everyday products should be stored away from food and medications, and to choose a storage place other than where foods and medicines are kept ([CPSC publication 382](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/382.pdf)). The EPA says not to spray or store these products near pet food or water dishes, and to keep them out of reach of both pets and children ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/pets/read-label-first-protect-your-pets)).

### Aerosols and heat {#aerosols-and-heat}

Poison Control says not to leave aerosol cans on a stove, radiator or furnace, in direct sunlight, or near other heat sources, because of toxic fumes and a fire risk. It adds that many polish and wax aerosols need protection from extreme heat ([Poison Control](https://www.poison.org/articles/spring-cleaning)).

## Setting this up in a small home (our suggestions) {#small-home-setup}

These are **our suggestions** for applying the rules above. They are not statements from the sources.

- **Pick one latched cabinet for cleaners** and keep it away from the shelf where food and medicines live. If your only cabinet is under the sink, that is a low cabinet, which Poison Control calls one of the most dangerous places, so a child-resistant latch matters most there.
- **Keep a short supply list.** The fewer products you own, the less there is to store, and it fits the one-product-at-a-time rule in our [never-mix guide](/guides/never-mix-cleaners-chart/).
- **Finish the day's cleaning by putting everything back and resealing it.** It is a good last step of the [10-minute reset](/guides/small-apartment-cleaning-schedule/#the-10-minute-daily-reset), and the [minimum day](/guides/minimum-day-cleaning-routine/) needs no cleaning product beyond dish soap.
- **If you have pets, check where their bowls sit** and keep cleaners and sprays well away from them.

## What the sources do not say {#what-the-sources-do-not-say}

- **No exact heights, sizes or product lists.** None of the pages gives a shelf height, a cabinet size or a list of which products go where.
- **No guidance on keeping cleaning products apart from each other.** The CDC's bleach page gives no storage guidance at all, and the pages we read do not say whether particular cleaners should be kept in separate places. We do not give a rule. The safest general point they make is not to mix products.
- **No advice on refilling spray bottles.** The sources say to keep products in original containers and never to use food or drink containers. They do not describe refilling or diluting, so we do not either. Follow each product's label.
- **No claim that a latch or a lock removes the risk.** They recommend them; they do not promise an outcome.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a lock, or is a latch enough?**
The CPSC says to keep household products in a cabinet or closet that locks or has a child-resistant latch. Poison Control recommends cabinets with child-resistant latches.

**Where do I store cleaners in a studio with one kitchen?**
The sources do not address studios. Their rules still apply: latched or locked, out of sight, away from food and medicines, and away from pet bowls. See our suggestions above.

**Can I move a cleaner into an old drink bottle to save space?**
No. The CPSC and the EPA both say never to put these products in containers used for food or drinks, and the EPA calls it extremely dangerous.

**What should I do if a child swallows a cleaner?**
Call the poison center at 1-800-222-1222 in the US, or use the web POISON CONTROL tool. Poison Control says it is free, confidential and open 24 hours a day.

**Is this a medical recommendation?**
No. It is general information about storage, taken from the sources listed below.
